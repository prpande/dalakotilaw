# Discharge Applications Are Not Empty Formalities: Why Courts Must Test the Prosecution Case Before Trial

In criminal litigation, one of the most important stages is often reached before the trial begins. This is the stage of discharge. A discharge application is not a technical objection. It is a substantive safeguard against unnecessary criminal trial where the prosecution material, even if accepted at its highest, does not disclose the basic ingredients of the alleged offence.

The recent discharge order passed by the Rouse Avenue Court, Delhi, in the Delhi Excise Policy case involving Arvind Kejriwal, Manish Sisodia and others has once again brought the law on discharge into focus. Irrespective of the political debate surrounding the case, the legal discussion is important because it reiterates a settled principle: a criminal court is not bound to mechanically frame charges merely because a chargesheet has been filed.

## The foundation: Union of India v. Prafulla Kumar Samal

The leading authority on discharge remains Union of India v. Prafulla Kumar Samal, where the Supreme Court explained the scope of Section 227 CrPC. The Court held that the Judge is "not a mere post office" to frame charges at the instance of the prosecution. The Court must apply judicial mind to the record and determine whether the material discloses sufficient ground for proceeding against the accused. At this stage, the Court may sift the evidence for the limited purpose of seeing whether a prima facie case exists, though it cannot conduct a full trial or weigh evidence as it would after evidence is recorded.

This distinction is crucial. The discharge court does not decide whether the accused is innocent. It decides whether the prosecution has placed enough legally relevant material to justify putting the accused through the burden of trial. The test is not proof beyond reasonable doubt, but it is also not mere suspicion. There must be a grave or strong suspicion arising from material which can legally support the ingredients of the alleged offence.

## Discharge under the BNSS

The same principle continues under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. In Sessions cases, Section 250 BNSS provides that the accused may file an application for discharge within sixty days from the date of commitment, and the Judge shall discharge the accused if there is no sufficient ground for proceeding.

For warrant cases instituted on a police report, Section 262 BNSS provides that the accused may file a discharge application within sixty days from the date of supply of documents under Section 230. The Magistrate must discharge the accused if, after considering the police report, documents, examination if any, and hearing both sides, the charge appears groundless. Thus, discharge is not a concession. It is a statutory stage where the Court must filter out cases that do not legally deserve trial.

## What the Delhi discharge order shows

In the Delhi Excise Policy case, the Rouse Avenue Court discharged all 23 accused persons, including Arvind Kejriwal and Manish Sisodia, holding that the material on record did not disclose even a prima facie case, much less grave suspicion, against the accused persons.

The Court reiterated that while the threshold at the charge stage is not proof beyond reasonable doubt, suspicion must still be strong and grounded in material, not conjecture or narrative convenience. The Court also observed that in cases based substantially on documents, file notings and policy decisions, the Court must see whether the documents, taken at face value, disclose the ingredients of the alleged offences and whether there is independent material showing mens rea, quid pro quo or abuse of office.

This reasoning is significant for all criminal cases, not only political or corruption cases. It shows that allegations cannot be allowed to proceed merely because they are strongly worded. A chargesheet must connect the accused to the offence through legally sustainable material.

## Why ingredient-wise scrutiny matters

A good discharge application should not merely say that the accused is innocent. It must show that the prosecution documents themselves do not satisfy the essential ingredients of the alleged offence.

For example, in a cheating case, the defence must test whether the prosecution material shows dishonest intention at the inception. In a criminal breach of trust case, the defence must test entrustment, dishonest misappropriation and loss. In conspiracy cases, the defence must test whether there is material showing meeting of minds, not merely association or communication. In corruption or abuse of office cases, the defence must test whether there is material showing dishonest intention, quid pro quo, unlawful gain or abuse of position.

This is where many discharge applications fail. They are drafted like final arguments after trial. The better approach is to take the prosecution's own record and demonstrate that, even if accepted as it is, the offence is not made out.

## The Court is not conducting a mini-trial

The prosecution often argues that all disputed issues must go to trial. That submission is correct only up to a point. At discharge stage, the Court cannot conduct a roving inquiry or decide disputed questions of fact. But it can and must see whether the prosecution material is legally capable of supporting the charge.

The Supreme Court in Prafulla Kumar Samal permits limited sifting of material. The recent Delhi order applies the same principle. The Court is not expected to blindly accept every prosecutorial inference if the documents themselves do not support the offence.

## Practical drafting lessons for discharge applications

A discharge application should be structured around the following points:

- First, identify the exact offence and its ingredients.
- Second, map the prosecution material against each ingredient.
- Third, show where the prosecution case rests on assumption, improvement, hearsay, contradiction or absence of corroboration.
- Fourth, avoid relying too heavily on defence material unless it is admitted, unimpeachable or forms part of the prosecution record.
- Fifth, emphasize that discharge is justified where the material creates only suspicion, but not grave suspicion.
- Sixth, show that continuation of trial would amount to harassment where the prosecution record itself does not disclose the offence.

## Why this stage protects justice

A criminal trial is itself a serious burden. It affects reputation, employment, liberty, family life and mental peace. Therefore, when the prosecution record does not cross the legal threshold, the accused should not be forced to undergo trial merely because an FIR was registered and a chargesheet was filed.

At the same time, discharge does not mean that courts should conduct a premature trial. It only means that courts must perform their statutory duty as a filter. The criminal process must be used to try legally sustainable accusations, not to test speculative narratives.

## Conclusion

The law on discharge is settled, but its correct application depends on careful drafting and judicial scrutiny. Union of India v. Prafulla Kumar Samal remains the foundation. The Delhi discharge order in the Arvind Kejriwal excise policy case is a recent reminder that the Court must examine whether the prosecution material discloses the ingredients of the offence and raises grave suspicion, not merely suspicion.

The order is presently under challenge before the Delhi High Court, and therefore its finality remains subject to further judicial scrutiny. However, as a discussion on discharge jurisprudence, it reinforces an important principle: criminal courts are not post offices for chargesheets. They are constitutional filters against unwarranted trials.
